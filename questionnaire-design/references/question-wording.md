# Question Wording & Response Options

This reference governs the wording of individual items and the construction of their response
sets. It serves **both modes** of the skill:

- **Create (Mode A):** apply these as design rules when drafting items.
- **Review (Mode B):** audit each item against them, then classify findings using Doctrine #4
  — a **defect** (Part I & II violations) always warrants a fix; a **divergence** (Part III
  scale choices) is a legitimate, tradition-dependent option and is *not* a flaw.

Source tags (e.g., *CCSG-QD*, *STATCAN-SMP*) resolve to full references in `source-map.md`.
Where a PT-BR illustration is given, it is drawn from *DATASENADO-DIR* and kept in Portuguese
because the contrast is in the wording itself.

## Part I — Universal item requirements (defect-grade)

These are not stylistic preferences; violating them threatens validity or comparability
regardless of methodological tradition. In review mode, a clear violation is a **defect**.

The benchmark, stated by *HARVARD-PSR*, is that an ideal item (a) measures the intended
construct, (b) does not measure other constructs, and (c) means the same thing to every
respondent. Each requirement below protects one of those three.

**1. One idea per item — no double-barreled questions.** An item must ask about a single
concept; combining two collapses two answers into one and makes the response uninterpretable
(*HARVARD-PSR*, *AAPOR-BP*, *CCSG-QD*, *NG-2006*, *DATASENADO-DIR* — PT-BR: *pergunta
"dois-em-um"*). Decompose into separate items.
- *No lugar de:* "Você concorda ou discorda que o governo federal, estadual e municipal
  investem o suficiente em educação?" → *Perguntar* três itens separados (federal; estadual;
  municipal). (*DATASENADO-DIR*)

**2. Neutrality — no leading, loaded, or presupposing wording.** Wording must not signal a
"correct" answer, embed emotionally charged terms, or assume a fact not yet established
(*AAPOR-BP*, *HARVARD-PSR*, *DATASENADO-DIR*). *DATASENADO-DIR* names four bias routes to
watch: implicit presuppositions, social-desirability pressure, suggestion/leading in the
stem, and respondent embarrassment. Sponsor or attribution cues ("President X's plan to…")
also bias responses (*HARVARD-PSR*).

**3. Plain, precise vocabulary — no jargon, no vague terms.** Use words the whole target
population shares; avoid technical terms, abbreviations, slang, and regionalisms (*all
sources*). Replace vague quantifiers ("frequently", "in general", "a maioria") with concrete
terms or explicit response categories (*HARVARD-PSR*, *NG-2006*, *DATASENADO-DIR*).
- *No lugar de:* "Qual é a sua frequência de uso de transporte público?" with vague options →
  give concrete options: *diariamente / algumas vezes por semana / raramente / nunca uso*.
  (*DATASENADO-DIR*)

**4. Avoid negatives and double negatives.** Negative stems and agree/disagree-with-a-negative
constructions confuse respondents (*DATASENADO-DIR*). Prefer the positive form ("Você
acompanha…?" rather than "Você não acompanha…?").

**5. Provide a reference frame.** Fix the time and object the respondent should consider;
otherwise different people answer different questions (*HARVARD-PSR*, *ASA-WIS*). For recall,
*DATASENADO-DIR* recommends a window of the last three months (up to twelve, depending on the
topic). Specify the object too ("the *national* economy" vs. "the economy").

**6. Keep recall realistic.** Do not tax memory for events never encoded or too distant
(*ASA-WIS*, *NG-2006*, *DATASENADO-DIR*). Decide the precision required in advance and signal
it ("roughly how many…" vs. an exact count) (*ASA-WIS*).

**7. Ask only what the respondent can answer.** Avoid topics outside the population's knowledge
or salience; simplify or screen instead (*DATASENADO-DIR*, *CCSG-QD*). In 3MC settings,
salience and taboo vary by culture (*CCSG-QD*).

**8. Avoid items with no variance.** Questions everyone answers identically ("Is health
important?") carry little information; reformulate to capture variability (e.g., "Which factor
do you consider most important…") (*DATASENADO-DIR*).

**9. Handle sensitive items with care.** Sensitive or self-incriminating topics invite
omission or distortion; reduce threat through self-administration, guaranteed confidentiality,
softened phrasing, or specialized techniques such as the randomized-response technique for a
few yes/no items (*CCSG-QD*, *ASA-WIS*, *DATASENADO-DIR*). Confidentiality assurances are also
an ethics requirement (see `ethics-and-standards.md`).

## Part II — Response options for closed items (defect-grade)

**10. Choose open vs. closed deliberately.** Closed items are easier to answer and analyze and
improve cross-respondent comparability, but constrain and can shape answers; open items
capture unanticipated content but require coding and are skipped more often (*HARVARD-PSR*,
*AAPOR-BP*, *CCSG-QD*, *NG-2006*). In quantitative instruments, default to closed; reserve open
fields for genuine qualitative depth (*DATASENADO-DIR*).

**11. Categories must be mutually exclusive.** No answer should fit two categories at once
(*ASA-WIS*, *AAPOR-BP*). Overlapping numeric ranges (e.g., "0–5" and "5–10") are a defect.

**12. Categories must be exhaustive.** Cover the full plausible range; add an "Other (specify
____)" when the set cannot be closed (*ASA-WIS*, *NG-2006*, *AAPOR-BP*, *HARVARD-PSR*). For
continua, word categories as non-overlapping ranges that span all possibilities (*HARVARD-PSR*).

**13. Options must fit the question.** Response options have to be answers to the stem actually
asked (a frequency question needs frequency options) (*DATASENADO-DIR*).

**14. Ensure ordinal options are truly ordered.** Every point must be unambiguously higher/
lower than its neighbors for all respondents ("a lot" vs. "many" is not clearly ordered)
(*HARVARD-PSR*).

**15. Control option order; rotate when appropriate.** Order induces primacy (visual modes:
first options favored) and recency (aural modes: last options favored) effects; rotating or
randomizing option order mitigates this (*HARVARD-PSR*, *AAPOR-BP*, *DATASENADO-DIR*,
*CCSG-QD*). For ranking tasks, *DATASENADO-DIR* advises ranking no more than five items and
always giving explicit instructions.

## Part III — Scale design: tradition-dependent choices (divergence-grade)

This is where the corpus genuinely disagrees, and where **Doctrine #1** applies: present the
options with the tradition behind each; do not impose a default. *STATCAN-SMP* states the
governing principle directly — for the number of categories, the neutral alternative, and the
"don't know"/"not applicable" option, *the right choice depends on the survey objective, the
item, the mode of collection, and the agency's own preference.* Treat the following as a menu
to choose from transparently, not as rules.

**A. Number of scale points.**
- *Options & traditions.* *HARVARD-PSR* suggests five to seven points usually works best;
  *NG-2006* treats five- or seven-point Likert scales as standard; *STATCAN-SMP* notes a rating
  scale may run from two points (agree/disagree) up to ten. Fewer points ease aural modes
  (telephone); more points offer finer gradation but raise interpretation and translation load.
- *Decision drivers:* mode, respondent burden, the precision the analysis needs, and
  comparability with prior instruments.

**B. Midpoint (neutral middle category).**
- *Options & traditions.* Offering a neutral middle (e.g., "neither agree nor disagree")
  lets ambivalent respondents report honestly; *HARVARD-PSR* holds that a middle category
  generally yields better data. The competing concern (*STATCAN-SMP*, citing Bishop, 1987):
  when a neutral option is offered, respondents gravitate to it, which can mask a real lean.
  *STATCAN-SMP* offers a mode-dependent compromise — include the neutral option in
  interviewer-administered modes but do not read it aloud, so it is recorded only when
  volunteered.
- *Decision drivers:* whether genuine neutrality is substantively meaningful for the construct;
  mode; tolerance for forcing vs. allowing a non-committal answer.

**C. "Don't know" / "No opinion" / "Not applicable".**
- *Options & traditions.* *NG-2006* argues that for **factual/knowledge** items a "Don't know"
  is necessary — omitting it forces guessing. *AAPOR-BP* includes "don't know"/"does not
  apply" *in some cases*. *HARVARD-PSR* prefers, on the telephone, to allow "Don't know" only
  as a *volunteered* response rather than an explicit read option. *STATCAN-SMP* makes "Not
  applicable" mandatory when some respondents cannot have an opinion (e.g., a service never
  used). The trade-off: an explicit DK reduces fabricated answers but can invite satisficing
  (an easy escape from effortful thought).
- *Decision drivers:* item type (knowledge vs. attitude), mode, and whether legitimate
  non-applicability exists.

**D. Agree–disagree (Likert) vs. item-specific (construct-specific) scales.**
- *Options & traditions.* Agree–disagree formats are widespread in applied and clinical
  research (used throughout *NG-2006* and in *DATASENADO-DIR* examples). However, the
  acquiescence-bias literature cautions against them: respondents tend to agree/say "yes" to
  please or to satisfice. *HARVARD-PSR* advises avoiding agree/disagree wording for this
  reason; *AAPOR-BP* flags the same "yes/agree" tendency; *CCSG-QD* documents acquiescence
  response style (ARS) and extreme response style (ERS) and shows they vary across cultural
  groups — a comparability threat in 3MC work. The alternative is an **item-specific** scale
  that names the dimension directly (e.g., "How satisfied… Very satisfied … Very dissatisfied"
  rather than "I am satisfied — agree/disagree").
- *Decision drivers:* exposure to acquiescence, cross-cultural comparability needs, and
  continuity with validated agree–disagree instruments.

**E. Scale labeling (full vs. partial) and balance.**
- *Options & traditions.* Label points with clear, unambiguous words (*HARVARD-PSR*).
  Fully labeling every point aids comprehension; labeling only the anchors is common for
  longer numeric scales. Scales should be **balanced** — symmetric positive and negative sides,
  with positive and negative items scored consistently high/low (*HARVARD-PSR*). *CCSG-QD*
  shows that unbalanced scales and inconsistent translation of a label (the point "fair") can
  distort and de-harmonize response distributions in cross-national data.
- *Decision drivers:* scale length, mode, and — critically in 3MC — translation equivalence of
  every label.

For any choice in Part III, in **create** mode present the options and let the user choose; in
**review** mode, record the author's choice as a *divergence* (name the competing tradition),
not a defect — unless the execution is internally broken (e.g., an unbalanced or non-ordinal
scale, a mistranslated label), which returns it to Part II/defect territory.

## Part IV — Mode and wording interaction (brief)

Mode constrains wording and response design: telephone favors short stems and few scale points
and is prone to recency effects; self-administered/web favors visual layout, can carry longer
option lists, reduces social-desirability pressure on sensitive items, and is prone to primacy
effects (*HARVARD-PSR*, *ASA-WIS*, *AAPOR-BP*, *CCSG-QD*, *STATCAN-SMP*). Decide the mode
*before* finalizing wording. For fuller mode treatment, see `instrument-types-and-modes.md`.

## Review-mode quick map: defect vs. divergence

| Observation in an item                                   | Classify as |
|----------------------------------------------------------|-------------|
| Two concepts in one stem (double-barreled)               | Defect      |
| Leading / loaded / presupposing wording                  | Defect      |
| Jargon, vague quantifier, or ambiguous term              | Defect      |
| Negative or double-negative stem                         | Defect      |
| Missing reference frame; unrealistic recall              | Defect      |
| Non-exclusive or non-exhaustive options                  | Defect      |
| Options that don't fit the stem; non-ordinal "ordinal"   | Defect      |
| Uncontrolled option order where it plausibly biases      | Defect      |
| Mistranslated / unbalanced scale (broken execution)      | Defect      |
| Chose 4 vs 5 vs 7 points; anchor-only labeling           | Divergence  |
| Included / omitted a neutral midpoint                    | Divergence  |
| Included / omitted explicit "Don't know"                 | Divergence  |
| Used agree–disagree vs. item-specific scale              | Divergence  |

A divergence is noted, attributed to its tradition, and — at most — questioned as to whether it
was deliberate. It is **not** rewritten. If an item has no defect, say so.
