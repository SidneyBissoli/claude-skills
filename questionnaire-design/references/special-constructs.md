# Special Constructs — Trust & Subjective Well-being

Some constructs have **validated, internationally recommended measures**, so designing items
for them is less an exercise in free wording (governed by `question-wording.md`) and more one of
**adopting or justifiably departing from** an established instrument. This reference covers the
two such constructs in the corpus — **subjective well-being** and **trust** — both via OECD
guidelines.

The governing rule here is a corollary of Doctrine #1 and #4: when a validated measure exists,
**reusing it verbatim buys comparability** with the wider evidence base; changing it may be a
legitimate, documented adaptation or may be a defect (a worse instrument *and* a loss of
comparability). In review mode, departing from a validated measure without reason is a weakness;
reusing the standard item is a strength to note.

## Part I — Subjective well-being (OECD-SWB 2025, 2nd ed.)

**Concept.** *OECD-SWB* keeps the broad definition first set out in the 2013 edition —
subjective well-being as good mental states, spanning the evaluations (positive and negative)
people make of their lives and their affective reactions to experience. It covers **three
components**:
- **Life evaluation** — a reflective assessment of one's life or some aspect of it.
- **Affect** — feelings, emotions and states; multidimensional (positive and negative), so no
  single item captures it in full.
- **Eudaimonia** — a sense of meaning, purpose, and good psychological functioning.

**Recommended measurement structure (2025, 2nd edition).** The second edition **streamlines**
the 2013 recommendations into a clearer module hierarchy:
- A **shortened core module of three priority measures**: a life-evaluation item (the
  **primary measure** — the single item to carry if space allows only one), a eudaimonia item,
  and a **pain** item. The core deliberately differs from 2013 in two ways: it **adds a pain
  measure** and **removes the three "yesterday"-recall affect questions**. Those affect items
  are *not* deprecated — they remain recommended and now live in the affect extended module; the
  change reduces respondent burden and widens adoption, since "yesterday"-recall items need
  large samples to be reliable. Pain is included as one well-performing example of an affective
  state, **not** as a stand-in for the whole affect construct.
- **Streamlined extended modules**, one per component (life evaluation, domain evaluation,
  affect, eudaimonia), plus a population mental-health module and time-use diary measurement —
  for producers needing more depth on a given component.
- A **new cross-cutting experimental module** with three experimental question banks: **balance
  and harmony**, **connection to future generations**, and **connection with nature** — concepts
  the OECD flags as under-measured. Treat these as experimental, not settled measures.

**Scale (unchanged in substance from 2013).** The recommended response format is a **0–10
numerical scale anchored by verbal labels at the extremes** (e.g. "not at all" / "completely"),
with the **interval points labelled numerically rather than verbally** — verbal interval labels
translate inconsistently across languages and are read unevenly. 0–10 numerical scales also
prime respondents to treat the intervals as equal-sized, an advantage over labelled Likert
scales, and travel better to phone modes where no visual aid is available. *Verify the exact
prompts, recall periods, and anchor wording against the original module boxes before fielding*
(integrity caveat) — this file gives the structure, not the verbatim batteries.

**Why the structure matters.** The three components are distinct constructs, not
interchangeable. Measuring only one (e.g., life satisfaction) and labelling it "well-being"
under-represents the concept — which is exactly why the core pairs life evaluation with
eudaimonia and an affect exemplar, and why fuller coverage routes to the extended modules.

> **Edition note.** This section reflects the **2025 second edition**, which supersedes the
> 2013 first edition as the current OECD recommendation. The 2013 edition remains the founding
> reference and is still cited in `source-map.md` for provenance; the core life-evaluation and
> eudaimonia items (Q1, Q2) carry over from it unchanged.

## Part II — Trust (OECD-Trust 2017; OECD-TrustSD 2024)

**Concept.** *OECD-Trust* defines trust as *a person's belief that another person or institution
will act consistently with their expectations of positive behaviour.* Trust is held **by
individuals**, but its **object** is wider, spanning both other people and institutions — which
gives the two standard dimensions:
- **Interpersonal trust** — trust in other people. There is clear consensus on using
  **generalised trust** as the preferred measure of interpersonal trust.
- **Institutional trust** — trust in institutions (government, parliament, the police, the
  judicial system, etc.). This covers **several sub-dimensions**, all of interest, so it is not
  reducible to a single item the way generalised trust is.

*OECD-Trust* notes a measurement subtlety worth carrying into design: "confidence in
institutions" and "trust in institutions" are not identical, and "confidence in an institution"
versus "confidence in an institution to act in the national interest" can diverge — so the exact
object and verb of a trust item materially change what is measured.

**Verified module structure (Annex B, 2017 Guidelines).** The Guidelines provide **five
prototype question modules**. **Module A — the "core module"** — is the one NSOs are encouraged
to include, and is intended to be used **in full and without significant amendment**. Its
verified structure:
- a **single generalised-trust question** (A1) as the preferred interpersonal-trust measure,
  plus a second interpersonal-trust item (A2);
- a small set of **institutional-trust items** measured on an **eleven-point 0–10 scale**
  anchored at 0 = "not at all" and 10 = "completely", asking respondents to base the answer on
  their general impression of each institution. The core module deliberately covers only a few
  institutions (e.g., the **national government**, the **parliament**, and the **police**);
  the further modules (B onward) extend to a broader range of institutions and are framed as
  **additions, not replacements**, for producers needing more depth.

This is the *structure and scale* — the complete item wording lives in the original annex and
should be taken from there (it is a published instrument; this file does not reproduce it whole).

**The 2024 technical update.** *OECD-TrustSD* (2024; documenting the 2023 data collection) is
the survey-design and technical documentation annex to the OECD Survey on Drivers of Trust in
Public Institutions. Its contribution is a **drivers framework** that decomposes institutional
trust into government **competences** (e.g., responsiveness and reliability of public services)
and **values** (e.g., integrity, openness, fairness), operationalised as survey modules. Because
this source extracts cleanly, its framework and item structure can be read directly from the
file; consult it as the **authoritative item source** when designing a public-trust instrument.
Two concrete, verified practices worth importing:
- **Order randomization to control response-order effects** — the survey randomized the order of
  the public-governance driver modules (notably *after* sensitive items such as integrity) and
  randomized response-option order in questions with many options (operationalizes
  `questionnaire-structure.md` order control and `question-wording.md` Part II-15).
- **An empirical DK pattern to anticipate** — integrity-related questions drew a *larger share
  of "don't know"* responses, which bears on how DK is handled for sensitive institutional items
  (`question-wording.md` Part III-C).

**Scale.** Trust items are typically measured on an **0–10** scale (consistent with the OECD
well-being house style). Confirm anchors and exact prompts against the source.

## Part III — Response biases these constructs share

Both OECD guidelines devote explicit attention to **response biases and heuristics** (each
discusses them in a dedicated section/overview). Self-reported subjective measures are
especially exposed to:
- **Scale/anchoring and response-style effects** — how respondents use the 0–10 range differs
  by person and (per `cross-cultural-adaptation.md`) by culture; acquiescence and extreme
  response styles apply.
- **Context and order effects** — a preceding item can prime a life-satisfaction or trust
  answer; placement matters (links to `questionnaire-structure.md`).
- **Item-specific nonresponse** — *OECD-Trust* tracks item-specific nonresponse rates (e.g., in
  the Gallup World Poll), a reminder that some trust items are skipped more than others.
- **Mode and social desirability** — trust in institutions and well-being reports can shift with
  mode and interviewer presence (links to `instrument-types-and-modes.md`).

The OECD treatment of these is the construct-specific instance of the general bias material in
`question-wording.md`; consult both.

## Part IV — Using validated measures well; review-mode notes

**Create mode.** If the objective involves well-being or trust, start from the OECD measures
rather than drafting from scratch: adopt the primary/core SWB module or the OECD trust items,
quote them accurately from the source, keep the recommended 0–10 scales, and document any
deliberate adaptation (with the cross-cultural cautions in `cross-cultural-adaptation.md`).
Reuse maximizes comparability with the international evidence base.

**Review mode** (Doctrine #4):

| Observation                                                          | Classify as |
|----------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------|
| "Well-being"/"trust" measured by one ad-hoc item, construct under-represented | Defect |
| Validated OECD item altered with no stated reason (comparability lost) | Defect/weakness |
| Trust object/verb ambiguous ("confidence in X" vs "to act in the national interest" conflated) | Defect |
| Bias/order/context risks for these items left unaddressed            | Defect/weakness |
| OECD measure reused verbatim with correct scale                      | Strength    |
| Deliberate, documented adaptation of a validated item for context    | Divergence (legitimate) |

Reusing the standard instrument is good practice and should be acknowledged, not "improved" for
its own sake. Recommend changes only for genuine construct-coverage, ambiguity, or bias defects.
