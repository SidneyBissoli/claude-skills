# Cross-Cultural Translation & Adaptation (3MC)

This reference covers building instruments that must work across languages, cultures, and
regions — what the literature calls **3MC** surveys (multinational, multicultural,
multiregional) — and, by extension, across time. *AAPOR/WAPOR-TF* stresses that these issues are
"also highly relevant to single-country multicultural and multiregional survey research, where
comparability is imperative" — directly pertinent to a country as regionally and educationally
diverse as Brazil, even when only Portuguese is involved.

It serves both modes: in **create**, when more than one language, region, or culture is in
scope, plan translation, adaptation, harmonization, and per-version pretesting from the start;
in **review**, check for equivalence threats, deliberate-and-tested adaptation, and adequate
documentation. Source tags resolve in `source-map.md`.

## Part I — The goal: comparability, equivalence, harmonization

The objective is **comparability** — that respondents in different populations (or periods)
answer what is, in measurement terms, the same question. *AAPOR/WAPOR-TF* is candid that
comparability "is highly complex," defined differently across disciplines, and that the extent
to which it can be achieved and assessed is itself a research question. A defining feature of
3MC work is **harmonization** — "a generic term for procedures aimed at achieving or at least
improving the comparability of answers" (*AAPOR/WAPOR-TF*, citing Granda et al.):
- **Input (ex-ante) harmonization** designs a common instrument/procedure before collection.
- **Output (ex-post) harmonization** converts differently-collected data to a common standard
  afterward.
Both must be documented and released as a methodological profile alongside the data
(*AAPOR/WAPOR-TF*). The total survey error perspective extends to 3MC as *comparison error*
(*AAPOR/WAPOR-TF*; *CCSG-QD*).

## Part II — Three design strategies (ASQT / ADQ / mixed)

*CCSG-QD* sets out three basic approaches to comparative question design:

- **ASQT — Ask the Same Question and Translate.** A common question set is asked of all
  populations, usually via a source questionnaire translated into target versions. Advantage:
  standardized stimuli. Disadvantage: a literal or near-literal translation may not be
  culturally suitable or even possible (the ESS could translate "belong to any particular
  religion" easily, but "difficulty walking several blocks" failed where neighborhoods are not
  organized in blocks).
- **ADQ — Ask Different Questions.** Deliberately different questions (or differently worded
  ones) to fit cultural/social needs — i.e., **adaptation** (Part IV).
- **Mixed.** Combines ASQT and ADQ.

Two related techniques (*CCSG-QD*):
- **Decentering** develops the same questions simultaneously in two languages with no
  source/target hierarchy, removing culture-specific elements from both — but it suits only
  two-language projects and is very work-intensive.
- **Anchoring vignettes** let researchers adjust for groups that interpret a scale differently,
  by having respondents rate hypothetical persons as well as themselves; they assume vignette
  equivalence and reporting consistency.

## Part III — Translation methods (a real divergence)

Apply Doctrine #1: the corpus contains two distinct traditions, and the skill should present
both rather than impose one.

- **Back-translation** (the older clinical/applied tradition). *NG-2006* describes
  translate–back-translate as "standard procedure": translate source→target, have an
  independent person back-translate target→source blind, then compare the original and
  back-translated source for discrepancies that flag translation problems.
- **Committee / team translation — TRAPD** (the 3MC tradition). *CCSG-QD* and *AAPOR/WAPOR-TF*
  favor a TRAPD model: **T**ranslation, **R**eview, **A**djudication, **P**retest,
  **D**ocumentation, with translators and reviewers working as a team. The 3MC literature treats
  committee approaches as superior to back-translation alone, because back-translation checks
  the *words* round-trip but not whether the target version *measures the same construct* for
  the target population.
- *Decision drivers:* stakes, number of languages, resources, and whether construct equivalence
  (not just lexical round-tripping) must be demonstrated. For a high-stakes or multi-language
  instrument, sole reliance on back-translation is a weakness the 3MC tradition would flag; for
  a small, low-stakes bilingual study, back-translation may be proportionate. Record the choice.

## Part IV — Adaptation (ADQ): when, why, and the common forms

*CCSG-AD* defines **adaptation** as the deliberate modification of a question to create a new
question (= ADQ). Translation and adaptation are usually entangled ("translation and
adaptation"), so the translation and adaptation teams should work closely together.

**The decisive principle.** In projects aiming to ask the same question of every population,
*CCSG-AD* states substantive adaptations should be made **only** if required to ensure
comparable measurement or to avoid some other important negative consequence — not merely
because someone thinks a version is "generally better." And there is a balance point: when the
changes required are so great that even the best adaptation cannot yield an equivalent question,
the original should be **discarded** for that context, not forced.

**Common forms of adaptation** (*CCSG-AD*), each illustrated:
- **System-driven** — units (Imperial/metric, Fahrenheit/Celsius), government structure ("head
  of government" → Prime Minister vs. President), laws/policies that exist in some contexts but
  not others (a minimum wage), and currency (straight conversion is rarely meaningful; some
  studies anchor to the local median wage).
- **Guide comprehension** — "Can you run 100 yards?" adapted in Vietnam to "100 yards or the
  distance of three light poles."
- **Conceptual coverage** — adding locally relevant components (e.g., culture-specific disease
  symptoms) so the construct is fully captured.
- **Cultural discourse norms** — politeness/deference markers; Korean honorifics; diglossia
  (written script vs. spoken delivery).
- **Cultural sensibilities** — the WISC-III Picture Completion used a missing object part in the
  Japanese version instead of a missing body part.
- **Design components** — reading direction; familiarity with visual scales (thermometers,
  ladders, faces); color symbolism (see also Part V and `question-wording.md`).
- **Lexicon and grammar** — "neither agree nor disagree" cannot be rendered element-for-element
  in Hebrew (it would read "no agree, no no agree"); ISSP uses a phrase corresponding to "in the
  middle."
- **Maintain or reduce difficulty** — simplify vocabulary, bolster instructions, or supplement
  scale presentation for populations unfamiliar with rating tasks.

**Process** (*CCSG-AD* guidelines): set adaptation policy/people/procedures; recruit a team
spanning adaptation knowledge, comparability needs, language proficiency, and cultural
knowledge; review the **source** for adaptation needs; review the **translated** instrument
for further needs; document each adaptation and its rationale; and **test adaptations with the
target population**.

## Part V — Response-scale and visual equivalence

Scales are an acute equivalence risk. *CCSG-QD* shows that inconsistent translation of a single
scale point ("fair") across countries produced different response distributions and
de-harmonized self-rated-health data, and that balanced vs. unbalanced scales behave
differently across cultures. *CCSG-AD* adds that scale labels may be untranslatable
element-for-element and that visual scale formats (faces, ladders, thermometers) carry cultural
familiarity assumptions. Treat every scale label and visual as something to test for
equivalence, not assume — this is the cross-cultural extension of `question-wording.md` Part
III-E.

## Part VI — Pretest every version; document everything

**Pretest each version.** Adaptation produces *new* questions, which must be pretested with the
target population (*CCSG-AD*); *CCSG-PT* requires pretesting every language version for
measurement and cross-cultural equivalence and warns that cognitive interviewing itself varies
across cultures (see `pretesting.md`). Adaptation left to the last moment leaves no time to
pretest — a sequencing failure.

**Document everything.** Record each adaptation and its rationale, and all input/output
harmonization, for version control, longitudinal continuity, and secondary analysts
(*CCSG-AD*, *AAPOR/WAPOR-TF*). Undocumented adaptation is a recurring weakness the literature
calls out.

## Review-mode map: defect vs. divergence

| Observation                                                         | Classify as |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------|
| Element-for-element translation that breaks meaning (e.g., a scale label) | Defect |
| Adapted/new items never pretested with the target population        | Defect      |
| Comparability claimed with no harmonization/equivalence assessment  | Defect      |
| Adaptation undocumented (no rationale recorded)                     | Defect/weakness |
| Adaptation made for "betterment," not measurement need, in an ASQ project | Defect (per CCSG-AD principle) |
| Strategy choice: ASQT vs. ADQ vs. mixed                             | Divergence  |
| Translation method: back-translation vs. TRAPD/committee            | Divergence (with 3MC caveat) |
| Decentering for a bilingual project                                 | Divergence  |

A deliberate, documented, tested adaptation is sound practice, not a flaw. Flag the absence of
deliberation, documentation, or testing — not the existence of adaptation.
